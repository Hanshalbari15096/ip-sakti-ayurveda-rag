================================================================================
INTERNATIONAL TREATIES - IP AND TRADITIONAL KNOWLEDGE (AYURVEDA RELEVANCE)
================================================================================

---
TOPIC: WIPO Treaty on Intellectual Property, Genetic Resources and Associated Traditional Knowledge (GRATK), 2024
JURISDICTION: International
SUMMARY_CHUNK: The WIPO GRATK Treaty was adopted on 24 May 2024 at a WIPO Diplomatic Conference in Geneva. It is the first WIPO treaty to protect traditional knowledge and genetic resources. Core provision: any patent application that is based on genetic resources and/or associated traditional knowledge must disclose the country of origin or source of the genetic resources, and the indigenous peoples or local communities who provided the traditional knowledge. Mandatory disclosure applies to applications filed after the treaty enters into force. Sanctions for non-disclosure are left to national law but must be proportionate. The treaty establishes a database of genetic resources and associated traditional knowledge for patent examiners. For Ayurveda: Indian exporters and researchers filing foreign patents on Ayurvedic formulations must disclose that the biological material and knowledge originate from India and its traditional knowledge holders; this complements India's own Section 10(4A) disclosure requirement under the Patents Act, 1970. The treaty opened for signature in 2024 and enters into force after 15 ratifications (achieved in 2025, with entry into force in 2026). India was among the earliest signatories and ratified it promptly, consistent with its long-standing position at WIPO's Intergovernmental Committee (IGC) demanding mandatory disclosure to prevent biopiracy of Ayurvedic and other traditional knowledge.

TOPIC: WIPO GRATK Treaty - Relevance for Ayurveda and Compliance Steps
JURISDICTION: International
SUMMARY_CHUNK: For Ayurvedic businesses and researchers, the WIPO GRATK Treaty (2024) means: (1) foreign patent applications based on Indian genetic resources (medicinal herbs like Ashwagandha, Turmeric, Neem) must disclose India as the country of origin. (2) Applications based on associated traditional knowledge must identify the indigenous peoples or local communities (e.g., Ayurvedic vaidya communities, BMC-documented knowledge) who provided it. (3) Failure to disclose enables sanctions under the filing country's national law, ranging from pre-grant observation procedures to post-grant revocation, as implemented nationally. (4) The treaty's examiner database will include TKDL-sourced prior art, strengthening defensive protection of Ayurvedic formulations. (5) Indian entities filing abroad should maintain sourcing documentation (herb provenance, ABS approvals from NBA/SBB, community consents) to support the disclosure. Compliance checklist: document the supply chain for every herb, retain PIC/MAT records, disclose origin in every PCT national-phase and direct filing, and run a TKDL prior-art search (https://tkdl.res.in) before drafting claims.

TOPIC: Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT) - Procedure and Ayurveda Relevance
JURISDICTION: International
SUMMARY_CHUNK: The Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT), administered by WIPO, allows a single international patent application to seek protection simultaneously in over 150 contracting states. Procedure: (1) Filing - a single international application in one receiving Office (for India, the Indian Patent Office acts as receiving Office) claiming priority from an earlier national filing (within 12 months). (2) International Search Report (ISR) and written opinion by an International Searching Authority - for Ayurvedic applications, the ISR routinely cites TKDL references and classical Ayurvedic texts, making a strong prior-art self-assessment essential before filing. (3) Publication at 18 months. (4) Optional International Preliminary Examination. (5) National phase entry - typically at 30/31 months from the priority date, when the applicant must pursue grant before each national/regional office and comply with local formalities, including GRATK-style disclosure requirements where applicable. Costs: international filing fee plus per-country national phase fees and translations. For Ayurveda: the PCT's international search is often the first hurdle where traditional-knowledge prior art is raised; applicants should draft claims that go beyond traditional use (novel extract, novel process, enhanced efficacy per Section 3(d) thinking) and disclose biological source origin.

TOPIC: Madrid System for International Trademark Registration
JURISDICTION: International
SUMMARY_CHUNK: The Madrid System (Madrid Agreement 1891 and Madrid Protocol 1989), administered by WIPO, lets a trademark holder file one international application, in one language, paying fees in one currency, to protect a mark in up to 130+ member countries covering 120+ countries. Procedure: (1) have a base national/regional trademark application or registration (for Indian Ayurvedic brands, an Indian TM application under the Trademarks Act, 1999). (2) File an international application through the Indian IP office as the Office of Origin to the International Bureau of WIPO. (3) WIPO registers the mark in the International Register, notifies the designated member countries, and each country examines under its own law (they can refuse within 12-18 months). (4) The international registration is valid 10 years and renewable indefinitely through WIPO. For Ayurvedic product brands (class 3 cosmetics, class 5 medicines, class 30/31 herbal foods), the Madrid System offers cost-effective international brand protection. Limitations: a "central attack" problem - if the base Indian mark is cancelled within 5 years, the international registration is transformed or cancelled.

TOPIC: Hague System for International Design Registration
JURISDICTION: International
SUMMARY_CHUNK: The Hague System (Hague Agreement 1925, including the 1999 Act), administered by WIPO, provides a single international application to register industrial designs in up to 96+ countries. Procedure: (1) File one international application directly with WIPO (or through the office of a contracting party) in one language, with one fee, covering multiple designs (up to 100 in one application). (2) WIPO records the registration in the International Register and publishes it. (3) Designated contracting parties examine under their own rules and can refuse within 6-12 months. (4) International registrations last 5 years and can be renewed in 5-year terms up to at least 15 years (term varies by country; India permits up to 10 years renewable once for 5 more). For Ayurvedic businesses: the shape of product containers, distinctive packaging, bottles for oils and churnas, cosmetic jars, and unique product appearances can be protected internationally via Hague. India acceded to the 1999 Act in 2019, enabling Indian applicants to use the system to export-brand Ayurvedic packaging designs worldwide.

TOPIC: Budapest Treaty on the International Recognition of the Deposit of Microorganisms
JURISDICTION: International
SUMMARY_CHUNK: The Budapest Treaty (1977), administered by WIPO, addresses a problem in patent law: for inventions involving microorganisms, a written description may not enable reproduction, so many patent offices require a deposit of the microorganism in a recognized culture collection. The Treaty provides that a deposit in any "International Depositary Authority" (IDA) - a culture collection recognized by WIPO - is recognized by all contracting patent offices, avoiding the need to deposit in every country. Procedure: (1) deposit the microorganism with an IDA before or at the filing date; (2) cite the deposit number in the patent application (e.g., under Indian law, Section 10(4)(d) requires the reference and deposit details where the invention involves a microorganism); (3) the deposit must remain available for the term of the patent. Relevance for Ayurveda: fermentation-based formulations, probiotic products (e.g., lactic ferment of herbal substrates), and microbial biotransformation processes used to produce bioactive compounds from herbs (e.g., microbial conversion in Shilajit processing or fermentation-derived actives) may require a Budapest deposit. Indian IDAs include the Microbial Type Culture Collection (MTCC) at IMTECH Chandigarh and the National Collection of Industrial Microorganisms (NCIM) at NCL Pune.

TOPIC: TRIPS Agreement Article 27.3(b) and Traditional Knowledge
JURISDICTION: International
SUMMARY_CHUNK: Article 27.3(b) of the TRIPS Agreement permits WTO members to exclude from patentability: (1) plants and animals other than microorganisms, and (2) essentially biological processes for the production of plants or animals other than non-biological and microbiological processes. Members must, however, provide for the protection of plant varieties either by patents, by an effective sui generis system (India chose the Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act, 2001), or a combination. This is the flexibility under which India structures Section 3 of its Patents Act. For Ayurveda, Article 27.3(b)-aligned exclusions let India refuse patents on known medicinal plants and traditional preparations while protecting genuinely novel, microbiological and non-biological innovations. The Doha Declaration and the ongoing TRIPS review (including the 2022 pandemic waiver precedent for flexibility) remain relevant contexts for how traditional-knowledge exclusions evolve at the WTO.

TOPIC: Nagoya Protocol - Access and Benefit-Sharing (International Framework)
JURISDICTION: International
SUMMARY_CHUNK: The Nagoya Protocol on Access to Genetic Resources and the Fair and Equitable Sharing of Benefits Arising from their Utilization (2010, in force 2014) supplements the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD, 1992). It establishes: (1) sovereign rights of countries over their genetic resources; (2) Prior Informed Consent (PIC) from the provider country's national authority before accessing genetic resources or associated traditional knowledge; (3) Mutually Agreed Terms (MAT) - negotiated benefit-sharing agreements covering monetary (royalties, upfront payments, milestone payments, licence fees) and non-monetary benefits (technology transfer, capacity building, joint research, joint IP ownership); (4) compliance measures in user countries, including checkpoints and Internationally Recognized Certificates of Compliance (IRCCs); (5) disclosure of origin obligations that link to patent disclosure requirements (now reinforced by the WIPO GRATK Treaty 2024). For Ayurvedic companies sourcing herbs from multiple countries, compliance means verifying ABS clearance in each provider country; India implements these obligations through the Biological Diversity Act, 2002 and the 2024 rules.

TOPIC: European Union Herbal Market Access - Traditional Herbal Registration and Food Supplements
JURISDICTION: International
SUMMARY_CHUNK: EU market access for Ayurvedic products follows two main routes: (1) Traditional Herbal Medicinal Products Directive (THMPD, Directive 2004/24/EC, amending 2001/83/EC) - a simplified registration allowing herbal medicines with 30 years of traditional use (15 of them within the EU) to be registered without full clinical trials, subject to a bibliographic safety review, quality (GMP) standards, and the HMPC (Committee on Herbal Medicinal Products) monograph system; registered products receive a "Traditional Herbal Registration" (THR) number and may carry mild efficacy claims for minor ailments only. (2) Food Supplements Directive (2002/46/EC) - vitamins, minerals and botanicals may be marketed as food supplements under national rules, with botanicals regulated divergently by member state; health claims must comply with the Nutrition and Health Claims Regulation (EC 1924/2006). Barriers for Ayurveda: heavy reliance on non-EU traditional use evidence, restricted active-ingredient lists (e.g., limits on ashwagandha under some national rules), contaminant limits for heavy metals, and Novel Food Regulation (EU 2015/2283) requirements for ingredients not used in the EU before May 1997. Strategy: position classical formulations as food supplements initially, pursue THR where monographs exist, and verify each herb's Novel Food status before export.

TOPIC: Biological Diversity (Amendment) Act and Rules - India 2024 Update
JURISDICTION: India
SUMMARY_CHUNK: The Biological Diversity (Amendment) Act, 2023 (in effect 2024) and the Biological Diversity Rules, 2024 streamline India's ABS regime. Key changes: (1) exemptions for AYUSH practitioners, Indian systems of medicine users, and cultivation of codified traditional-medicine plants from SBB intimation/approval for certain uses; (2) "codified traditional knowledge" including Ayurveda texts is recognized, easing access to listed medicinal plants; (3) decriminalization - offences are now dealt with via penalties rather than criminal prosecution (fines up to Rs 1 crore for serious violations), with adjudication by designated officers replacing criminal courts; (4) simplified benefit-sharing: the 2024 rules introduce ad valorem benefit-sharing ranges (0.2% to 1.0% of annual gross ex-factory sale price for manufacturers using biological resources; higher ranges for foreign entities) instead of purely case-by-case negotiation; (5) industry registration with SBB/NBA moved to an online portal with deemed approvals for statutory timelines; (6) foreign entities and NRI-linked access still require NBA prior approval, and transfers of research results abroad remain regulated. For Ayurvedic manufacturers: register with the SBB, use the online ABS portal, maintain herb-sourcing records, and apply the ad valorem benefit-sharing rates in the Biological Diversity Rules, 2024.

TOPIC: Patents (Amendment) Rules, 2024 (India) - Procedural Changes
JURISDICTION: India
SUMMARY_CHUNK: The Patents (Amendment) Rules, 2024 (effective 15 March 2024) introduce procedural changes relevant to Ayurvedic/pharmaceutical applicants: (1) reduced time to request examination (48 months, down from 48 months from priority) with expedited examination tracks; (2) certificate of inventorship added; (3) a uniform 3-month period for responding to office actions; (4) reduced pre-grant opposition processing - the Controller may now dispose of pre-grant oppositions via review rather than a full Opposition Board reference (rules amended to allow "review" by the Controller on request), and anonymous pre-grant oppositions are discouraged (identity and interest of the opposer must be disclosed); (5) divisional applications can only claim subject matter from the parent application as filed; (6) reintroduced condonation of delay provisions with fees for restoring abandoned applications; (7) accelerated processing for startups, women applicants, and government institutions (already existing) extended. Combined with the pre-existing expedited examination route for the pharma sector, applicants for novel Ayurvedic formulations (PAMs, phytopharmaceuticals) can now expect materially faster grant timelines; classical-knowledge-based claims remain barred under Sections 3(c), 3(d) and 3(p) of the Patents Act, 1970.

TOPIC: Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954 (India)
JURISDICTION: India
SUMMARY_CHUNK: The Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954 (DMRDA) controls the advertisement of drugs and magic remedies in India. It prohibits: (1) advertisements that give false impressions or make false claims for a drug; (2) advertisements for magic remedies in the treatment of diseases and disorders (a "magic remedy" includes talismans, mantras, kavachas, and any other purported supernatural means); (3) advertisements for the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment or prevention of 50+ listed diseases and conditions (including appendicitis, blindness, blood poisoning, cancer, cataract, diabetes, epilepsy, feeble-mindedness, hernia, infertility, leprosy, liver disease, paralysis, tuberculosis, tumours, and others) for any drug. For Ayurvedic products this means: marketing claims that an Ayurvedic formulation cures or treats listed diseases are prohibited, regardless of classical textual support for the use; products may only make permitted claims consistent with their regulatory category (drug claims for approved medicines must still comply with the DMRDA's prohibited disease list). Penalties: imprisonment up to 6 months (first offence) or up to 1 year for subsequent offences, and fines. The Act works alongside the Consumer Protection Act, 2019's misleading-advertisement provisions and CCSPA guidelines, 2022 for curative claims made in ads for Ayurvedic medicines.

TOPIC: DPCO - Drugs (Prices Control) Order, 2013 (India)
JURISDICTION: India
SUMMARY_CHUNK: The Drugs (Prices Control) Order, 2013 (DPCO), issued under Section 3 of the Essential Commodities Act, 1955, regulates the prices of scheduled (essential) medicines and monitors non-scheduled formulations. The National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority (NPPA) administers it. Key provisions for Ayurvedic products: (1) Ayurvedic, Unani, and Siddha medicines are NOT scheduled under DPCO 2013, meaning their prices are not directly fixed by the NPPA; (2) however, ayurvedic formulations crossing the 10% annual price-increase threshold come under monitoring, and retail price increases are monitored for non-scheduled allopathic formulations at 10% per year; (3) phytopharmaceutical drugs approved by DCGI may face price scrutiny; (4) all manufacturers must comply with the provisions on packing sizes, MRP printing and seasonal/point-of-purchase display rules; (5) if any herb-based product includes an allopathic scheduled molecule (in the rare case of a fixed-dose combination), DPCO price caps apply to the molecule part. The 2024 draft "Digital SDK" for NPPA compliance and the price-monitoring mechanisms are expected to eventually extend observation to high-volume ayurvedic OTC brands. For Ayurvedic startups: no NPPA price approval is needed for pure ayurvedic formulations, but MRP labelling rules and consumer-protection pricing norms still apply.

TOPIC: FSSAI Ayurveda Aahar (Ayurvedic Food) Regulations - 2022 (India)
JURISDICTION: India
SUMMARY_CHUNK: The Food Safety and Standards (Ayurveda Aahar) Regulations, 2022 govern foods prepared in accordance with Ayurvedic texts and recipes, the first time "Ayurveda Aahar" (Ayurvedic food) has been recognized as a distinct regulatory category under the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006. Scope: covers proprietary Ayurvedic foods (e.g., Chyawanprash-like pastes marketed as food, herbal drinks, ayurvedic supplementary foods) based on formulations from authoritative Ayurvedic texts, where the food claim is nutritive/health-supportive rather than curative. Requirements: (1) manufacture only under FSSAI licence with GMP/hygiene conditions; (2) "Ayurveda Aahar" labelling with a logo specified by FSSAI, plus the mandatory disclaimer "THIS AYURVEDA AAHAR IS NOT A MEDICINE"; (3) ingredients must comply with FSSAI's list of approved herbs and botanicals with permitted daily amounts; (4) claims are restricted to the categories allowed in the FSSAI (Nutraceuticals, Health Supplements) Regulations, and disease-cure claims are prohibited; (5) additives and contaminants limits (including heavy metals) apply as per FSSAI standards. Category codes under the FSSAI licensing system (Kind of Business "Ayurveda Aahar") were created in 2022. For Ayurvedic companies, this opens a compliant "food" route for classical-recipe products that make no disease-treatment claims.

TOPIC: TKDL Prior-Art Search Guide (Practical)
JURISDICTION: India
SUMMARY_CHUNK: The Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL, https://tkdl.res.in) is a searchable prior-art database of Indian traditional medicine (Ayurveda, Unani, Siddha, Yoga) containing transcription of over 350,000 formulations from 150+ classical texts into five languages (English, French, German, Spanish, Japanese) in a patent-searchable format. How to use it: (1) before drafting any patent on an herbal formulation, search TKDL (access is available to the public in browsing mode; patent offices access the full database under NDAs) for the core herbs, indications, and processes; (2) TKDL prior art is citable in pre-grant oppositions, revocations, and international searches (TKDL references have been cited in 100+ patent applications and were central to the turmeric, neem, and basmati victories); (3) companies exporting Ayurvedic products can use TKDL documentation to certify traditional provenance; (4) TKDL is maintained jointly by CSIR and the Ministry of AYUSH; (5) note that TKDL is defensive - it prevents others' patents but does not grant rights to the knowledge itself. Practical steps: search by herb name (Sanskrit and botanical), disease/indication, and classical text; save search reports; cite TKDL accessions in any opposition or freedom-to-operate analysis.

TOPIC: Human Escalation - IP Attorney and AYUSH Support Contacts
JURISDICTION: India
SUMMARY_CHUNK: When a question exceeds what an AI assistant can responsibly answer (e.g., litigation strategy, patent drafting, ABS contract negotiation), escalate to qualified humans: (1) Registered Patent Agents and IP attorneys - searchable via the Indian Patent Office's agent registry (https://ipindia.gov.in) and the IP Attorneys Association; look for agents experienced in pharmaceuticals/traditional medicine. (2) AYUSH-Industry facilitation - the Ministry of AYUSH runs the AYUSH Entrepreneurship/Start-up promotion programs; the AYUSH Park and incubation centres (e.g., Patanjali Yogpeeth incubation, ARIIA-ranked institutes) provide regulatory guidance. (3) National Biodiversity Authority (https://nba.india.gov.in) for ABS approvals and clarifications. (4) Geographical Indications Registry, Chennai (https://ipindiaservices.gov.in/GIRPublic/App) for GI registration guidance. (5) CSIR-TKDL unit for traditional-knowledge verification and access agreements. (6) Free legal aid: NALSA (https://nalsa.gov.in) provides legal services to eligible applicants. (7) Trade bodies: ADMA (Ayurvedic Drug Manufacturers Association) and AYUSH industry chambers offer member support on regulatory and IP matters. Escalation is recommended for: patent drafting/filing, opposition or litigation, benefit-sharing contract negotiation, DCGI/FSSAI licensing strategy, and GI applications.
