================================================================================
IP STATUTES - INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY LAWS FOR AYURVEDA
================================================================================

---
TOPIC: Patents Act, 1970 (India)
JURISDICTION: India
SUMMARY_CHUNK: The Patents Act, 1970 governs patent protection in India. Section 3(c) excludes from patentability "the mere discovery of a new form of a known substance which does not result in the enhancement of the known efficacy" or "the mere discovery of any new property or new use for a known substance." This section is particularly relevant for Ayurvedic patents, as it prevents the patenting of new formulations of known Ayurvedic herbs unless they demonstrate enhanced efficacy. Section 3(p) also excludes from patentability "any traditional knowledge" including Ayurvedic knowledge.

---
TOPIC: Section 3(d) of the Patents Act, 1970
JURISDICTION: India
SUMMARY_CHUNK: Section 3(d) of the Indian Patents Act, 1970 is a specific anti-evergreening provision that excludes from patentability "the mere discovery of a new form of a known substance which does not result in the enhancement of the known efficacy of that substance" or "the mere discovery of any new property or new use for a known substance, or the mere use of a known process, machine or apparatus unless such known process results in a new product or employs at least one new reactant." For Ayurvedic herbs and formulations, Section 3(d) is critical because it prevents the patenting of new forms (such as salts, esters, polymorphs, or new formulations) of already-known Ayurvedic ingredients unless the applicant demonstrates significantly enhanced therapeutic efficacy. The Supreme Court of India relied on Section 3(d) in the landmark Novartis v. Union of India (2013) case to reject the patent application for Glivec (imatinib mesylate), setting a strong precedent that has been used to refuse patents on incremental modifications of known drugs and herbal formulations.

TOPIC: Biopiracy Penalties in India
JURISDICTION: India
SUMMARY_CHUNK: Penalties for biopiracy in India are governed primarily by the Biological Diversity Act, 2002 and the Patents Act, 1970. Under Section 55 of the Biological Diversity Act, any person who contravenes the provisions of the Act (including obtaining biological resources or traditional knowledge without prior approval of the National Biodiversity Authority) is punishable with imprisonment for a term which may extend to five years, or with a fine which may extend to ten lakh rupees, or with both. Additional penalties include confiscation of the biological material and cancellation of any patent obtained through unauthorized access. Under the Patents Act, revocation of patents granted on the basis of fraudulent or incorrect disclosure is possible. The Indian government has also used diplomatic and international mechanisms, including filing oppositions at patent offices abroad, to challenge biopiracy cases like turmeric and neem patents.

TOPIC: Patents (Amendment) Act, 2005 (India)
JURISDICTION: India
SUMMARY_CHUNK: The Patents (Amendment) Act, 2005 introduced Section 3(p) which explicitly excludes "any traditional knowledge" from patentability. This amendment was made to comply with the TRIPS Agreement and to prevent biopiracy of Indian traditional knowledge including Ayurvedic knowledge. The amendment also introduced Section 10(4A) which requires patent applicants to disclose the source and geographical origin of biological resources used in the invention.

---
TOPIC: Patents (Amendment) Act, 2024 (India)
JURISDICTION: India
SUMMARY_CHUNK: The Patents (Amendment) Act, 2024 introduced several changes including: (1) a new Section 3(t) which excludes from patentability "any invention which is likely to contravene public order or morality or cause serious prejudice to human, animal or plant life or health or to the environment," (2) a new Section 3(u) which excludes "any process for the medicinal, surgical, curative, prophylactic, diagnostic, therapeutic or other treatment of human beings to render them free of disease or to enhance their physiological conditions or efficiency," and (3) a new Section 15A which provides for expedited examination of patent applications in certain fields including pharmaceuticals and Ayurvedic drugs.

TOPIC: Geographical Indications Act, 1999 (India)
JURISDICTION: India
SUMMARY_CHUNK: The Geographical Indications Act, 1999 provides protection for geographical indications associated with products that have a specific geographical origin and possess qualities due to that origin. GI registration is valid for 10 years and can be renewed indefinitely. The Act provides both civil and criminal remedies for infringement. The GI Registry maintains a database of registered GIs and can assist in enforcement actions.

---
TOPIC: Trademarks Act, 1999 (India)
JURISDICTION: India
SUMMARY_CHUNK: The Trademarks Act, 1999 protects trademarks associated with goods and services. For Ayurvedic products, trademarks can protect brand names, logos, and packaging designs. Section 9(1)(c) prohibits registration of trademarks that are "of the nature or quality, quantity, intended purpose, value, geographical origin or time of production of the goods" or that "may cause confusion or deception." Section 9(2)(c) prohibits registration of trademarks that "tend to become common names in the trade or language."

TOPIC: Copyright Act, 1957 (India)
JURISDICTION: India
SUMMARY_CHUNK: The Copyright Act, 1957 protects original literary, dramatic, musical, and artistic works. For Ayurveda, this can protect original Ayurvedic texts, commentaries, and educational materials. However, copyright does not protect ideas, procedures, methods of operation, or mathematical concepts. Traditional Ayurvedic knowledge that has been passed down for generations is generally not protected by copyright as it lacks the requirement of originality and the author's identity is often unknown.

TOPIC: Designs Act, 2000 (India)
JURISDICTION: India
SUMMARY_CHUNK: The Designs Act, 2000 protects the design of a product, which is defined as any shape, configuration, pattern, or ornament applied to a product by any industrial process. For Ayurvedic products, designs can protect the appearance of Ayurvedic packaging, containers, and product designs. Design registration is valid for 10 years and can be renewed for an additional 5 years.

TOPIC: Plant Varieties Protection Act, 2001 (India)
JURISDICTION: India
SUMMARY_CHUNK: The Plant Varieties Protection and Farmers' Rights Act, 2001 protects new plant varieties and the rights of farmers. For Ayurvedic herbs, this Act can protect new varieties of medicinal plants that are distinct, uniform, and stable. The Act also protects farmers' rights including the right to save, use, sow, re-sell, share, and exchange seeds of protected varieties. The Act establishes the Plant Variety Protection and Farmers' Rights Authority to administer the registration system.

TOPIC: Biological Diversity Act, 2002 (India)
JURISDICTION: India
SUMMARY_CHUNK: The Biological Diversity Act, 2002 governs access to biological resources and associated traditional knowledge in India. It requires that any transfer of biological resources for commercial use must obtain prior approval from the State Biodiversity Board. The Act also requires that any transfer of traditional knowledge to foreign entities must obtain approval from the National Biodiversity Authority (NBA). The Act implements the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) and the Nagoya Protocol on Access and Benefit-Sharing.

TOPIC: TRIPS Agreement and Ayurveda
JURISDICTION: International
SUMMARY_CHUNK: The TRIPS Agreement (Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights) is an international agreement administered by the World Trade Organization (WTO). It sets minimum standards for intellectual property protection that all WTO members must implement. For Ayurveda, TRIPS allows countries to exclude from patentability "diagnostic, therapeutic and surgical methods for the treatment of humans or animals." Article 27.2 also allows countries to exclude inventions from patentability if their commercial exploitation would be contrary to public order or morality. Article 27.3(b) requires WTO members to provide protection for plant varieties either by patents, an effective sui generis system, or any combination thereof. The TRIPS flexibilities used by India include Section 3(d), Section 3(p), and compulsory licensing provisions that are particularly relevant for protecting Ayurvedic traditional knowledge from biopiracy and ensuring access to medicines.

TOPIC: Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL) - Statutory Basis
JURISDICTION: India
SUMMARY_CHUNK: The Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL) was established in 2001 as a joint initiative between the Council of Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR) and the Ministry of AYUSH. TKDL is a defensive IP protection tool that contains over 35 million pages of India's traditional medical knowledge transcribed from authoritative Ayurvedic, Unani, Siddha, and Yoga texts. The database translates traditional Sanskrit, Arabic, Persian, and Urdu knowledge into a modern patent-searchable format in five international languages (English, German, French, Spanish, Japanese). TKDL operates under the Council of Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR) Act and is integrated with patent offices worldwide through non-disclosure agreements with the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO). It has successfully defended against patents on turmeric (wound healing), neem (anti-fungal), and other Ayurvedic formulations, preventing hundreds of biopiracy attempts globally. TKDL access is granted to patent examiners who search for prior art before granting patents.

TOPIC: Patents for Ayurvedic Formulations - Eligibility
JURISDICTION: India
SUMMARY_CHUNK: Patents for Ayurvedic formulations in India are subject to specific restrictions under the Patents Act, 1970. Section 3(d) prohibits patents on new forms of known substances unless enhanced efficacy is demonstrated. Section 3(p) excludes any traditional knowledge from patentability. Section 3(c) excludes the mere discovery of new properties or new uses for known substances. However, novel formulations (proprietary Ayurvedic medicines) containing new combinations, processes, or delivery methods may be patentable if they demonstrate: (1) novelty - not part of prior art, (2) inventive step - non-obvious to a person skilled in the art, and (3) industrial application. Patents on isolated compounds from herbs (e.g., specific withanolides from Ashwagandha) may be granted if the isolation process is novel. Patent applications for Ayurvedic products must disclose the source of biological material under Section 10(4A) and comply with the Biological Diversity Act, 2002 by obtaining prior approval from the National Biodiversity Authority.

TOPIC: IP Rights for Ayurveda - Comprehensive Overview
JURISDICTION: India
SUMMARY_CHUNK: Intellectual Property rights protecting Ayurveda include: (1) Patents (Patents Act, 1970) - for novel formulations, processes, isolated compounds; subject to Section 3(d) and Section 3(p). (2) Trademarks (Trademarks Act, 1999) - for brand names, logos, packaging. (3) Geographical Indications (GI Act, 1999) - for region-specific products. (4) Copyright (Copyright Act, 1957) - for original texts, commentaries, databases. (5) Designs (Designs Act, 2000) - for packaging and container designs. (6) Plant Variety Protection (PVPFRA, 2001) - for new medicinal plant varieties. (7) Trade Secrets - for traditional manufacturing processes and formulations. (8) TKDL Documentation - defensive protection for traditional knowledge. (9) Biological Diversity Act, 2002 - regulates access and benefit sharing. (10) Convention on Biological Diversity and Nagoya Protocol - international framework. A multi-pronged IP strategy combining these rights provides comprehensive protection for Ayurvedic innovations, products, and traditional knowledge.

TOPIC: Section 3(p) of the Patents Act - Traditional Knowledge Exclusion
JURISDICTION: India
SUMMARY_CHUNK: Section 3(p) of the Patents Act, 1970 (introduced by the 2005 amendment) explicitly excludes "any traditional knowledge" from patentability. This provision was designed to prevent the patenting of traditional Ayurvedic, Unani, and Siddha knowledge by foreign entities seeking to monopolize traditional formulations. The definition of traditional knowledge includes knowledge originating from Indian traditional systems of medicine, agriculture, and culture. Section 3(p) does not exclude all traditional knowledge-based innovations - novel processes or applications that go beyond existing traditional knowledge may still be patentable. The TKDL database serves as a key reference for what constitutes traditional knowledge. Section 3(p) implements India's obligations under Article 27.3(b) of TRIPS while protecting indigenous knowledge from biopiracy. Combined with Section 3(d) requiring enhanced efficacy, Section 3(p) provides strong protection against patents on incremental modifications of traditional Ayurvedic formulations.

TOPIC: Geographical Indications for Traditional Medicine
JURISDICTION: India
SUMMARY_CHUNK: Geographical Indications (GIs) for traditional Ayurvedic medicine in India protect products with specific geographical origins and associated quality, reputation, or characteristics. GI registration under the Geographical Indications Act, 1999 is valid for 10 years (renewable indefinitely). Famous Ayurvedic-related GIs include: Darjeeling Tea (West Bengal), Naga Mircha (Nagaland), Guggulu (Rajasthan/Gujarat), Saffron (Jammu & Kashmir), and various Kerala-based formulations. GI protection provides: (1) exclusive right to use the GI name for products from the registered area, (2) prevention of unauthorized use by producers outside the region, (3) civil remedies including injunctions and damages, (4) criminal penalties (imprisonment up to 6 months, fines up to Rs. 2 lakhs), (5) customs enforcement against counterfeit goods. For traditional medicine, GIs protect the link between specific geographical regions and their traditional cultivation, harvesting, and manufacturing practices. GI registration is administered by the GI Registry in Chennai.
